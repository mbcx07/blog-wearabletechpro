The Health Wearable Showdown: Apple Watch vs Samsung vs Garmin vs Fitbit and the AR Glasses Factor

2026-07-29 · Health Tech · Tags: The, health tech

Wearable health technology has evolved far beyond simple step counting. Today's devices are sophisticated medical-grade tools that monitor heart rhythms, blood oxygen levels, sleep stages, and even stress markers. As a reviewer who has tested dozens of wearables, I've seen the landscape shift dramatically—smartwatches now rival dedicated fitness trackers, and AR glasses are beginning to enter the health monitoring arena. But with so many options, how do you choose? In this comprehensive guide, I'll break down the strengths and weaknesses of the leading smartwatch brands, explore the best fitness trackers, and examine the emerging role of AR glasses in personal health. Whether you're an athlete, a chronic condition patient, or just someone who wants to stay on top of their wellness, this comparison will help you make an informed decision.

The Smartwatch Heavyweights: Apple, Samsung, Garmin, and Fitbit

When it comes to smartwatches, four names dominate the market: Apple, Samsung, Garmin, and Fitbit. Each has carved out a unique niche in health monitoring, and the differences are more pronounced than ever.

Apple Watch Series 9 and Ultra 2 remain the gold standard for seamless integration with iOS. The ECG app is FDA-cleared, and the blood oxygen sensor is highly accurate. The new temperature sensing feature for cycle tracking is a game-changer for women's health. However, the battery life is still a weak point—you'll be charging daily, which can be a dealbreaker for continuous sleep tracking.

Samsung's Galaxy Watch 6 and Watch 6 Classic offer comparable features for Android users. The BioActive sensor combines heart rate, ECG, and body composition analysis in one compact package. Samsung has also improved its sleep coaching, and the rotating bezel on the Classic is a tactile delight. But the health app ecosystem is less mature than Apple's, and some metrics like blood pressure monitoring are only available in select regions.

Garmin is the undisputed king of endurance sports. The Forerunner 965 and Fenix 7 Pro offer advanced training metrics like VO2 max, training load, and recovery time. Their GPS accuracy is unmatched, and battery life can stretch for weeks. For serious athletes, Garmin's data depth is invaluable. However, the smartwatch features are basic—no LTE on most models, and the interface feels utilitarian.

Fitbit, now part of Google, focuses on holistic wellness. The Sense 2 and Versa 4 excel at stress tracking with their electrodermal activity (EDA) sensor and mindfulness tools. Fitbit's sleep score is one of the most intuitive in the industry. But the hardware is aging, and the app has become cluttered with premium subscription prompts. Still, for everyday users who want a simple, reliable health companion, Fitbit remains a solid choice.

Fitness Trackers: Beyond Step Counting

While smartwatches get the headlines, dedicated fitness trackers are quietly becoming more sophisticated. The latest generation of trackers offers many of the same health sensors as smartwatches but in a lighter, more discreet form factor.

The Whoop 4.0 is a subscription-based tracker that focuses on recovery and strain. It has no screen, which forces you to rely on the app for insights. Its daily recovery score, based on heart rate variability (HRV), resting heart rate, and sleep quality, is a favorite among athletes and biohackers. The new Whoop 5.0 is rumored to add blood pressure monitoring, which would be a major leap.

Another standout is the Oura Ring, which packs a surprising amount of sensors into a jewelry-like ring. It tracks sleep stages, body temperature, and heart rate with impressive accuracy. The ring's form factor is ideal for those who find wristbands uncomfortable. However, it's not for everyone—the ring is expensive, and you need to subscribe to access all data.

For budget-conscious users, the Xiaomi Smart Band 8 Pro offers a bright AMOLED display, continuous heart rate and SpO2 monitoring, and 14-day battery life for under $50. It lacks the advanced recovery metrics of Whoop or Oura, but it's an excellent entry point into serious health tracking.

What sets these trackers apart is their focus on passive monitoring. They don't buzz with notifications; they quietly collect data that you can analyze later. This approach is often more effective for long-term health insights because it reduces the temptation to constantly check your wrist.

AR Glasses: The Next Frontier in Health Monitoring

Augmented reality (AR) glasses are typically associated with gaming and productivity, but they're beginning to make inroads into health tech. The idea is to overlay health data directly into your field of vision, creating a hands-free, always-available dashboard.

One of the most promising examples is the Xreal Air 2, which can display real-time heart rate, pace, and route information when paired with a compatible smartwatch. Runners and cyclists can see their metrics without breaking stride. The glasses are lightweight and comfortable, but they still require a wired connection to a smartphone or watch, which limits their practicality.

More advanced are the Vuzix Blade 2, which include a built-in heart rate sensor and step counter. These glasses are designed for industrial and medical use, but they hint at a future where AR glasses replace wrist-worn devices entirely. Imagine walking into a doctor's office and having your vitals displayed in front of you—that's the promise of AR health wearables.

However, the technology is still in its infancy. Battery life is short, the field of view is limited, and the health sensors are not as accurate as dedicated wearables. For now, AR glasses are best seen as a complement to your smartwatch or tracker, not a replacement. But as the hardware improves and the software becomes more sophisticated, we could see a shift in how we interact with our health data.

Health Monitoring Wearables: Blood Pressure, ECG, and More

Beyond the big brands, there's a growing category of specialized health wearables that target specific conditions. These devices are often more accurate than general-purpose smartwatches and can provide clinical-grade data.

Blood pressure monitoring is a prime example. The Omron HeartGuide is a wrist-worn blood pressure monitor that uses oscillometric technology to measure systolic and diastolic pressure. It's FDA-cleared and has been validated in clinical trials. While it's bulkier than a typical smartwatch, it's a lifesaver for people with hypertension who need regular readings.

ECG monitoring is now standard on many smartwatches, but the Withings ScanWatch goes a step further. It combines a traditional analog watch with a medical-grade ECG and a pulse oximeter. The ScanWatch can detect atrial fibrillation and has received CE marking as a medical device. Its 30-day battery life is a huge advantage over smartwatches.

For continuous glucose monitoring (CGM), the Dexcom G7 and Abbott FreeStyle Libre 3 are wearable sensors that transmit glucose levels to a smartphone app. While primarily used by diabetics, these devices are gaining popularity among health enthusiasts who want to understand how their diet affects their blood sugar. The data can be eye-opening, revealing how certain foods cause spikes and crashes.

These specialized wearables are not for everyone, but they demonstrate the potential of wearable health tech to move beyond fitness and into true medical management. As sensors become smaller and more accurate, we'll likely see these features integrated into mainstream devices.

How to Choose the Right Wearable for Your Health Goals

With so many options, the decision can be overwhelming. The key is to start with your health goals and then match them to the device's strengths.

  • For athletes and serious fitness enthusiasts: Garmin is the clear winner. Its training metrics, GPS accuracy, and battery life are unmatched. The Forerunner 965 is my top pick for runners, while the Fenix 7 Pro is ideal for multi-sport athletes.
  • For everyday health tracking and smart features: The Apple Watch Series 9 is the best all-rounder if you're an iPhone user. Its ECG, blood oxygen, and temperature sensing are top-notch, and the app ecosystem is second to none. For Android users, the Samsung Galaxy Watch 6 offers similar features.
  • For stress and sleep management: Fitbit Sense 2 and Whoop 4.0 are excellent choices. Fitbit's EDA sensor and sleep score are intuitive, while Whoop's recovery metrics are more data-driven. If you prefer a ring, the Oura Ring is a great alternative.
  • For medical conditions: If you need blood pressure monitoring, the Omron HeartGuide is essential. For ECG and AFib detection, the Withings ScanWatch is a reliable option. For diabetes, a CGM like Dexcom G7 is a must.
  • For early adopters: AR glasses like Xreal Air 2 are fun to experiment with, but they're not yet practical for daily health monitoring. Wait for the next generation.

Remember that no single device does everything perfectly. Many users pair a smartwatch with a dedicated tracker or a specialized medical device. The goal is to find a system that you'll actually wear consistently—because the best wearable is the one that stays on your wrist (or finger, or face).

Conclusion

The wearable health tech landscape is more exciting than ever. Smartwatches from Apple, Samsung, Garmin, and Fitbit have become powerful health companions, while dedicated fitness trackers and specialized medical wearables offer even deeper insights. AR glasses are on the horizon, promising to make health data even more accessible. As you navigate this crowded market, focus on your personal health goals and choose a device that aligns with your lifestyle. Whether you're a marathon runner, a busy professional, or someone managing a chronic condition, there's a wearable out there that can help you take control of your health. The future of health monitoring is not just on your wrist—it's in your pocket, on your finger, and soon, right before your eyes.
