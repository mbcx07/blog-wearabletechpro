The Fitness Tracker Face-Off: Apple Watch vs. Samsung vs. Garmin vs. Fitbit—and the AR Glasses Twist

2026-06-05 · Fitness Trackers · Tags: wearabletechpro, The, fitness trackers

Fitness trackers have come a long way from the simple pedometers of the early 2000s. Today, they are sophisticated health hubs that monitor everything from heart rate variability to blood oxygen saturation, and they’re increasingly integrated with augmented reality (AR) glasses to deliver real-time, hands-free insights. As a wearable tech reviewer, I’ve spent countless hours testing the latest devices from Apple, Samsung, Garmin, and Fitbit, and I’ve also explored how AR glasses are beginning to complement these wrist-worn powerhouses. In this comprehensive guide, I’ll break down the strengths and weaknesses of each major player, dive into the advanced health metrics that matter, and help you decide which wearable—or combination of wearables—is right for your fitness journey.

The Big Four: Apple, Samsung, Garmin, and Fitbit at a Glance

When it comes to fitness tracking, four brands dominate the market: Apple, Samsung, Garmin, and Fitbit. Each has its own philosophy, design language, and ecosystem, and the differences are more than skin deep. The Apple Watch Series 9 and Ultra 2 are the gold standard for smartwatch integration, offering seamless iPhone connectivity, a stunning always-on display, and a robust app store. Samsung’s Galaxy Watch 6 and Watch 6 Classic bring a similar level of polish to Android users, with a rotating bezel that’s both functional and nostalgic. Garmin, on the other hand, focuses almost exclusively on fitness and outdoor enthusiasts, with models like the Forerunner 265 and Fenix 7 Pro offering multi-band GPS, advanced training metrics, and battery life that can stretch for weeks. Fitbit, now under Google’s umbrella, has pivoted toward health and wellness, with the Sense 2 and Charge 6 emphasizing stress management, sleep tracking, and ECG capabilities.

What sets these devices apart isn’t just the hardware—it’s the software and the data they provide. Apple’s Health app aggregates data from multiple sources, while Samsung’s Samsung Health offers a more gamified experience. Garmin’s Connect app is a treasure trove of training analytics, and Fitbit’s app is arguably the most user-friendly for beginners. But when it comes to raw fitness tracking accuracy, Garmin often takes the crown, especially for runners and cyclists who need precise GPS and heart rate data. Apple and Samsung are closing the gap, but they still lag in battery life and outdoor-specific features.

Beyond Steps: Advanced Health Metrics That Matter

Modern fitness trackers are no longer just about counting steps. They now measure heart rate variability (HRV), blood oxygen saturation (SpO2), skin temperature, and even electrocardiogram (ECG) readings. These metrics provide a window into your overall health and recovery, helping you optimize your training and avoid overtraining. For example, Garmin’s Body Battery feature uses HRV, stress, and sleep data to estimate your energy reserves, while Fitbit’s Daily Readiness Score does something similar. Apple Watch’s Vitals app (introduced in watchOS 10) tracks overnight metrics and alerts you to anomalies, and Samsung’s BioActive sensor measures everything from body composition to blood pressure (with a compatible cuff).

But not all metrics are created equal. While SpO2 is useful for altitude training and sleep apnea detection, it’s not a substitute for medical-grade pulse oximetry. Similarly, ECG on a smartwatch can detect atrial fibrillation, but it’s not a replacement for a clinical ECG. As a reviewer, I always advise users to treat these features as wellness tools, not diagnostic devices. That said, the trend toward more comprehensive health monitoring is undeniable. Garmin’s new HRM-Pro Plus chest strap, for instance, adds running dynamics and can be paired with a watch for even more accurate data. Fitbit’s Sense 2 includes an EDA sensor for stress tracking, and Apple Watch Series 9 has a temperature sensor that can track ovulation and detect fevers.

The AR Glasses Wildcard: A New Way to See Your Health

While smartwatches and fitness trackers have become ubiquitous, AR glasses are emerging as the next frontier in wearable health tech. Devices like the Xreal Air 2 and the Vuzix Blade 2 can project real-time data directly into your field of view, allowing you to see your heart rate, pace, and distance without glancing at your wrist. This is a game-changer for runners and cyclists who need to keep their eyes on the road. But the integration goes beyond simple data display. Some AR glasses, like the upcoming Meta Ray-Ban Stories, can pair with your smartwatch to provide audio coaching and haptic feedback, creating a truly immersive training experience.

Imagine going for a run and seeing a virtual pacer alongside you, or having your AR glasses highlight the optimal route based on your heart rate zones. That’s the promise of AR in fitness. While the technology is still in its infancy, several companies are already experimenting with AR-enhanced workouts. For example, the HoloLens 2 has been used in corporate wellness programs to guide employees through yoga and stretching routines. And with the recent launch of the Apple Vision Pro, which includes a dedicated fitness app, the line between AR and fitness is blurring. However, these devices are still expensive and bulky, and they’re not yet practical for everyday workouts. For now, the most practical use of AR glasses is as a companion to your smartwatch, providing a heads-up display for your most critical metrics.

Health Monitoring Wearables: The Next Frontier

Beyond fitness tracking, wearables are increasingly becoming medical-grade devices. The Apple Watch Series 9 and Ultra 2 can perform an ECG, detect falls, and monitor for irregular heart rhythms. Samsung’s Galaxy Watch 6 can measure blood pressure (with a cuff) and track sleep apnea. Garmin’s Venu 3 includes a skin temperature sensor and advanced sleep coaching. Fitbit’s Sense 2 offers stress management and EDA scans. But the real breakthrough is on the horizon: non-invasive glucose monitoring. Companies like Dexcom and Abbott are developing continuous glucose monitors (CGMs) that can be worn on the wrist, and while they’re not yet integrated into mainstream smartwatches, the potential is enormous. Imagine being able to see how your blood sugar responds to different foods in real time, without finger pricks.

Another area of innovation is blood pressure monitoring. While current smartwatches require a traditional cuff for calibration, researchers are working on optical sensors that can measure blood pressure continuously. The technology is still in the lab, but it’s a matter of time before it hits the market. In the meantime, wearables are getting better at detecting conditions like atrial fibrillation, which can lead to stroke if left untreated. The Apple Heart Study, which enrolled over 400,000 participants, showed that the Apple Watch’s irregular rhythm notification can identify AFib with high accuracy. Similarly, Fitbit’s heart rhythm assessment has been cleared by the FDA. These are not just gimmicks—they’re life-saving tools.

Choosing the Right Wearable for Your Fitness Goals

With so many options, how do you choose? It all comes down to your specific needs and preferences. Here’s a quick breakdown to help you decide:

  • For the Apple ecosystem enthusiast: The Apple Watch Series 9 or Ultra 2 is the obvious choice. It offers the best integration with iPhone, a vast app library, and top-notch health features. The Ultra 2 is ideal for extreme athletes, with a titanium case, dual-frequency GPS, and a 36-hour battery life.
  • For Android users who want a smartwatch: Samsung’s Galaxy Watch 6 is the best all-rounder, with a beautiful display, rotating bezel, and comprehensive health tracking. The Watch 6 Classic adds a physical bezel for a more traditional look.
  • For serious athletes and outdoor adventurers: Garmin is the undisputed king. The Forerunner 265 is perfect for runners, while the Fenix 7 Pro is a rugged multi-sport watch with mapping, music, and solar charging. Battery life ranges from 7 to 30 days, depending on the model.
  • For beginners and health-focused users: Fitbit’s Sense 2 or Charge 6 are excellent choices. They’re easy to use, have long battery life (up to 7 days), and offer a free app with no subscription required. The Sense 2 also includes a built-in ECG and EDA sensor.
  • For those who want AR glasses: If you’re interested in AR, consider the Xreal Air 2 or Vuzix Blade 2 as a companion to your smartwatch. They’re not standalone fitness trackers, but they can display your watch’s data in your field of view, making workouts more immersive.

Ultimately, the best wearable is the one you’ll actually wear every day. Battery life, comfort, and ease of use are just as important as the number of sensors. I’ve seen people buy a Garmin Fenix 7 only to abandon it because it’s too bulky for daily wear, while others swear by the Apple Watch’s convenience. My advice is to try before you buy, and don’t be afraid to mix and match—a smartwatch for daily health tracking and a dedicated fitness tracker for workouts can be a winning combo.

Conclusion

The fitness tracker landscape is more exciting than ever. Apple, Samsung, Garmin, and Fitbit are pushing the boundaries of what’s possible, and AR glasses are adding a new dimension to how we interact with our health data. Whether you’re a casual walker or a marathon runner, there’s a wearable out there that can help you achieve your goals. The key is to focus on the metrics that matter to you, and to use these devices as tools for empowerment, not obsession. As we look to the future, the integration of wearables with AR and AI will only deepen, making health monitoring more seamless and personalized. So, which wearable will you choose? The decision is yours, but one thing is certain: the era of passive step counting is over. Welcome to the age of proactive health.
